1. Introduction

In the last decade the infrastructure for electric vehicle (EV) charging experienced enormous growth. Yet, there still exists a scarcity of EV infrastructure.

Most EVs are charged at homes. EV owners find it inconvenient to go to commercial charging stations, often due to a lack of charger availability.

That scarcity is also unevenly distributed. California alone holds roughly two and a half times the station count of the next largest state.

This research seeks to understand how the cost of EV charging varies around the United States. We find significant and substantial effects for geographic region, facility type, charging network, and for measures of variable cost (electricity), supply (number of charging cables), and demand (vehicle miles traveled).

Why this matters

I. Electric vehicles represent the future of driving. We are entering a tipping point at 5 to 10 percent of new automotive sales, expected to reach 25 percent in three to five years. Auto manufacturers are aligning their infrastructure to develop more EVs.

II. Climate change. Transportation accounts for 14 percent of global emissions (EPA, 2010). Driving accounts for 45 percent of transportation emissions (IEA, 2018). Automotives therefore contribute roughly 6 percent of total global emissions, and constitute 15 percent of total U.S. emissions.

III. Other motivations. EVs promote cleaner air and fewer pollutant-related deaths. And gas prices are too damn high.

3. Research Question & Addition to Literature

The origin of this research can be traced back to experiences and observations in the realm of electric vehicle mobility. A firsthand understanding of the dynamics and mechanisms at play motivated deeper and more nuanced questions. One such question, which was inspired by a Chief Financial Officer's curiosity about the government's role, involves the effects of policy shifts on the EV industry. Specifically, we aim to understand how changes in government subsidies could potentially influence EV charging prices.

Beyond this, our research also seeks to delve into the broader factors that may impact EV charging prices, assessing their respective degrees of influence. This pursuit requires us to engage with a variety of factors including market trends, technological advances, infrastructural capabilities, and regulatory frameworks, among others. Our goal is not only to identify these factors but also to quantify their impact and establish a comprehensive understanding of the interplay among them.

Another important facet of our study focuses on developing empirical measures to effectively gauge supply and demand for EV charging. To achieve this, we propose to leverage public data and construct robust models that could capture the market dynamics accurately.

This research serves as an important addition to the existing literature by extending our understanding of consumer behavior in the EV market. It seeks to contribute a new dimension by analyzing the impact of network effects on EV charging, an aspect previously overlooked in scholarly discourse.

4. Limitations & Constraints

Sources

While conducting our research, we faced several limitations and constraints, primarily pertaining to our data sources and their quality. Our sources included numerous reputable agencies and organizations, such as the U.S. Government, the Department of Energy (DoE) with its sub-agencies like the Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy (EERE), the Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC), and the Energy Information Administration (EIA).

We also sourced data from the Department of Transportation (DoT), including the Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) and Federal Highway Administration (FHA), the Census Bureau, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), and several commercial entities like EVgo, ChargePoint, and PlugShare. Additionally, the Clean Cities Coalition, the International Energy Agency, the Argonne National Lab's Center for Transportation Research, and AAA also provided valuable data for our research.

Quality

Despite these varied sources, we encountered some challenges related to the quality of the data. For instance, the AFDC price data had inherent limitations owing to its nature. Prices are recorded as free text, in whatever format the operator chose to publish, so a single column mixes per-kWh rates, per-minute rates, parking fees, session caps and tiered structures.

There was also a notable lack of granularity in non-station geographic data. Our budget constraints prevented us from acquiring microgeographic data tools, which could have otherwise enhanced the detail and depth of our analysis. Furthermore, the data collected were essentially snapshots taken at specific points in time and did not provide a continuous, time-series perspective.

Another significant constraint was the minimal variation observed in the dependent variable prices, which was coupled with large variations across different states. This discrepancy complicated our analyses and limited the extent of the conclusions we could draw. Despite these limitations, we endeavored to provide the most comprehensive insights possible with the resources at our disposal.

5. Description of Variables

Two dependent variables are modeled, both in logs: the total charging price, defined as the approximate cost to charge a 2023 Nissan Leaf far enough to drive 50 miles, and the fixed fee charged simply to plug in. The independent variables cover geography, facility type, charging network, and three continuous measures for supply, variable cost and demand.

6. Models

Model 1 log(TotalChargingPrice) = U.S.Region + Facility + ChargingNetwork  + log(Supply) + log(VariableCost) + log(Demand)
Model 2 log(ChargingPriceFixedFee) = U.S.State + Facility + ChargingNetwork  + log(Supply) + log(VariableCost) + log(Demand)

Both specifications are log-log in the continuous terms, so the coefficients on supply, variable cost and demand read directly as elasticities.

Three groups of candidate regressors are mutually correlated: the geography measures, the network characteristics, and the demand measures. Rather than admit all of them, one representative was selected from each group by comparing explanatory power, the share of levels reaching significance, and the highest variance inflation factor.

Our findings reveal an interesting dichotomy in the EV charging landscape. Specifically, we observed that the pricing strategy of Blink, a leading EV charging provider, is comparatively expensive, while Tesla's strategy appears to be more cost-efficient. This could be attributed to Tesla's business model, which aims to incentivize consumers to buy their electric vehicles by keeping the charging costs relatively low. This acts as a key differentiator in the marketplace and could significantly sway the purchasing decision of potential EV buyers.

Another significant observation is the influence of geographical location, facility type, and network effects on the charging prices. Charging costs can vary based on whether the charging station is located in a metropolitan area, a suburban area, or a rural region. The type of facility, whether a commercial establishment, a public parking area, or a residential complex, also impacts the price. Additionally, we found that network effects play a pivotal role in determining pricing. The density and distribution of charging stations in a region can influence consumer behavior and, consequently, the pricing strategies of EV charging providers.

Our study further unraveled the counterbalancing forces of supply and demand on charging prices. An increase in supply, symbolized by the availability of more charging stations, tends to decrease the price. An increase in demand, represented by a growing number of EV users, tends to drive the price upward. This interplay showcases the fundamental market dynamics at work in the EV charging landscape.

Furthermore, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our results. The conclusions remained largely consistent even when we removed California observations, Tesla observations, and other potentially influential data points.

However, we must underline that while analyzing this complex system, issues such as multicollinearity and heteroskedasticity must be closely monitored. The residual plots below make the second concern visible.

Multicollinearity, the correlation among independent variables, can distort the model's estimations. Heteroskedasticity, where the variability of the error terms is unequal across different levels of the explanatory variables, can complicate the interpretation of results. Rigorous statistical checks are therefore crucial to maintain the integrity of the research findings.

8. Conclusion & Further Research

In conclusion, our research offers valuable insights into the current landscape of the EV charging market. As we look towards the future, the potential growth of the EV market and its corresponding charging infrastructure is promising. Several critical factors will drive this growth, including technological advancements, changing consumer preferences, and supportive government policies.

One specific area we suggest for further research is the effect of government subsidies on charging prices. As government policy is an influential factor in the EV market, a detailed understanding of its impact could offer valuable insights for both policy makers and market participants. This includes investigating how changes in subsidies could impact the competitive landscape and influence the pricing strategies of charging service providers.

A comprehensive cost-benefit analysis of EV policies could also prove beneficial, particularly in assessing the existence and scale of positive externalities. This could help quantify the societal benefits of adopting EVs and, by extension, influence the scale and design of future policies.

Another interesting area for further research could be the examination of two-part tariffs in the context of EV charging. This refers to a pricing strategy where consumers pay a fixed fee for access and a variable fee based on usage. Understanding how this approach could be applied to EV charging may offer new opportunities for market differentiation and consumer engagement.

Lastly, a deeper dive into the network effects and the practice of tying could provide valuable insights. Network effects refer to the phenomenon where the value of a product or service increases with the number of users, while tying is a strategy where a company sells two products together. Given the growth and dynamism of the EV market, these areas could offer fascinating avenues for future exploration.
